# Beyond Socialism and Capitalism

Socialism asks how value should be distributed.

Capitalism asks who owns value.

Inclusionism asks where value came from.

This distinction changes everything.

Both socialism and capitalism often begin after value has already been created. Their primary disagreement concerns how value should be allocated once it exists.

Inclusionism begins earlier.

It asks how value emerged in the first place.

No value is created in isolation.

Every innovation depends upon prior knowledge.

Every business depends upon workers, customers, infrastructure, institutions, culture, and countless forms of participation.

Every intelligence depends upon relationships.

Value is therefore relational before it becomes economic.

The challenge of civilization is not merely producing value.

The challenge is attributing value.

When attribution fails, legitimacy fails.

When legitimacy fails, fairness becomes contested.

When fairness becomes contested, belonging deteriorates.

This is why Inclusionism places relational attribution at the center of political economy.

The objective is neither equal distribution nor winner-take-all distribution.

The objective is increasingly accurate distribution.

As societies become more capable of measuring contribution, they should become more capable of recognizing contribution.

The history of civilization can be understood as a gradual expansion in humanity's ability to identify who participated in the creation of value.

Inclusionism therefore seeks a future in which value is distributed according to increasingly sophisticated understandings of contribution, participation, and relational impact.

The goal is not perfect equality.

The goal is legitimate attribution.

Fairness emerges from legitimacy.

Belonging emerges from fairness.

And legitimacy emerges from recognition of contribution.
